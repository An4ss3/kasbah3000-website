Kasbah3000's food gardens use agroecology, compost and varieties suited to Morocco's seasons. They complement produce from neighbouring farms rather than pursuing isolated self-sufficiency.

A shared kitchen and processing spaces support communal meals, preservation and the passing on of recipes. Residents learn to plan crops, reduce waste and make healthy, affordable food part of everyday life.

## Agroforestry

Agroforestry is a land-use system where trees, crops and sometimes animals are integrated on the same land, creating a resilient, multi-layered ecosystem. Unlike monoculture farming, which depletes soil and water, agroforestry works with nature rather than against it. Trees provide shade, fix nutrients, retain water and prevent erosion while offering fruit, wood, medicine and habitat — all as farming continues around and beneath them.

Five core principles guide the design:

1. **Stratification** — different plants occupy different canopy levels, from tall trees through medium shrubs to ground crops, just as in a forest. This makes more productive use of sunlight, space and soil.
2. **Diversity** — a mix of species such as fig, pomegranate, lemon and legumes provides resilience to pests, disease and climate variation.
3. **Soil enrichment** — deep-rooted trees draw up nutrients, while leguminous plants such as clover or acacia fix nitrogen into the soil.
4. **Water efficiency** — shade from trees reduces evaporation, and organic matter improves water retention in the soil.
5. **Microclimate regulation** — trees act as windbreaks and provide shade, protecting crops from heat stress and soil from erosion.

Kasbah3000 goes beyond conventional agroforestry by drawing on syntropic farming, the method developed by Ernst Götsch that mimics forest succession by planting in dense, diverse layers which evolve over time to increase soil health and productivity, and on regenerative agriculture practices that rebuild soil organic matter and make land more fertile year after year.

### A worked design

For a settlement hosting six families, a one-hectare zone in front of the earth-built homes is planted as a layered food forest:

- **Canopy layer** — 25 fig trees and 20 orange trees in rows spaced 6–8 metres apart, providing shade, attracting pollinators and slowing evaporation.
- **Mid layer** — 35 pomegranate bushes between the figs, drought-tolerant and fruit-heavy, plus 20 lemon trees along the southern edge for wind buffering and citrus harvest.
- **Lower layer** — rotational beds of potatoes grown beneath fig shade and mulched with dried citrus prunings, with mint, garlic and clover as companion plants for pest control and nitrogen fixing.

Ground preparation matters as much as planting. Contour swales catch rainwater during winter and direct it underground, while greywater from the communal kitchen and wash basins is filtered through gravel and slowly released at root level. There are no chemical inputs: fertility comes from compost toilets, plant matter and animal bedding, with soil built through annual pruning, leaf mulch and layered planting. A solar-powered pump supports drip irrigation during the dry months.

A grove of lemons and figs is not just a garden — it is a cooling system, a carbon sink, a living pantry and a place of peace. The trees cool the walls, the greywater feeds the forest, the compost feeds the soil, and the people harvest, observe and adapt.
