Kasbah3000 begins with efficiency: building orientation, climate-appropriate insulation, natural ventilation and efficient equipment reduce consumption without reducing comfort.

Solar installations then produce electricity and hot water close to where they are used. Shared monitoring, carefully sized storage and priority for essential needs strengthen the site's autonomy while retaining a resilient connection to existing networks.

## Solar thermal water heating fields

In Morocco, and especially in rural areas, heating water is often costly and depends on gas bottles or unreliable electricity. Yet the country enjoys over 3,000 hours of sunshine each year — an untapped source of free energy.

Unlike solar panels that produce electricity, solar thermal collectors absorb the sun's heat to warm water directly. In a solar thermal field, multiple collectors feed into a large, insulated storage tank. A fluid — usually water or a special antifreeze solution — flows through pipes in the collectors and absorbs the heat. Hot water is then piped to nearby homes for showers, washing and cleaning, day and night, with a conventional heater available to boost the temperature when needed.

A worked scenario for a village of around 20 traditional kasbah-style homes near Azilal, in the High Atlas, sizes the system at 120 m² of flat-plate collectors — roughly 6 m² per house — feeding one well-insulated 6,000-litre tank. Annual output is approximately 30,000 kWh of thermal energy, about 1,500 kWh per family.

|                             | With solar thermal | Without solar thermal    |
| --------------------------- | ------------------ | ------------------------ |
| Gas bottles per family/year | ~2–3               | 12                       |
| Cost per family/year        | ~100–150 MAD       | ~600 MAD                 |
| CO₂ emissions (village)     | ~1–2 tons/year     | ~7–8 tons/year           |
| System lifespan             | 20–30 years        | ~7–10 years (for heater) |

Sized and maintained well, a single field can cover 80% of a village's hot water demand, cut carbon emissions by over 7 tons per year, and pay for itself in fuel savings within 5–7 years while lasting 20+ years with minimal upkeep.

The field is installed on shared land next to the housing units, and local cooperatives can own and maintain it. Training local technicians and plumbers creates jobs and keeps the system reliable for decades. Combined with rainwater harvesting and greywater recycling, it contributes to a resilient, low-cost community.

## Redox flow batteries

Kasbah3000 relies on intermittent renewable sources — photovoltaic panels and vertical axis wind turbines — so flexible storage is essential to smooth supply and meet round-the-clock demand. Redox flow batteries account for 50% of the total energy storage capacity of the system.

Redox flow batteries store energy in liquid electrolytes held in separate external tanks. The electrolytes are pumped into a central electrochemical cell where a membrane separates them but allows ions to pass. During charging, electricity from the panels or turbines drives a chemical reaction that changes the oxidation states of the electrolytes to store energy; during discharge the reaction reverses, converting the stored chemical energy back into electricity.

Because storage and conversion are decoupled, energy capacity depends on the size of the tanks and power output on the size of the cell stack — the two can be scaled independently, a significant advantage at community scale. The chemistry brings:

- High reliability and deep cycle life, often exceeding 10,000 cycles
- Non-flammable, water-based electrolytes that are safer and environmentally friendly
- Easy scalability, suited to expanding rural or peri-urban installations
- Fast response times, supporting both short-term power balancing and long-term storage

Energy harvested during peak sun and wind periods is dispatched steadily to LED lighting, irrigation pumps, food storage facilities, workshops and e-bike charging stations.

The batteries do not work alone. Hydrogen electrolysis converts surplus electricity into hydrogen gas, stored for backup energy or cooking gas and particularly useful for seasonal storage or prolonged low generation. Pumped storage hydropower, using elevation differences and recycled water systems, provides gravitational storage that helps manage stability and peak demand. As needs grow, the batteries can be expanded simply by increasing electrolyte tank capacity.
