Buildings are oriented to benefit from shade, thermal mass and natural ventilation. Courtyards, patios, solar screens and transitional spaces reinterpret proven ideas from kasbahs and medinas.

Modular design allows homes to evolve with families and generations. Simulation and in-use measurements will guide improvements so that real performance remains central to architectural decisions.

## Passive ventilation systems

Passive ventilation regulates indoor climate without relying on electricity. Rather than consuming large amounts of energy to force cold air inside, it uses natural airflow, pressure differences and temperature gradients to circulate air — a system designed to work with the environment rather than against it.

The underlying principle is simple: warm air rises and cool air sinks. By strategically placing openings such as windows, vents, wind catchers and skylights, buildings guide fresh, cooler air inside while pushing stale, warmer air out. Three techniques work together:

- **Cross-ventilation** — cool air enters from one side of a room and exits through another opening, creating a steady breeze.
- **Stack effect** — hot air escapes through higher openings such as skylights or chimneys, which pulls cooler air in from lower openings.
- **Thermal mass and shading** — materials that absorb heat during the day and release it at night work in tandem with airflow to maintain comfortable temperatures.

### Cooling tubes

Cooling tubes, also called earth tubes or earth-air heat exchangers, are underground pipes that naturally temper incoming air before it enters a building. Warm outside air is drawn into intake openings located slightly above the ground and travels through tubes buried in the soil. Because underground temperatures remain relatively stable — cooler in summer, warmer in winter — the soil cools the air in hot months and pre-warms it in cold ones. The tempered air enters through vents, and warm air rises and escapes through higher vents or chimneys, creating a convection loop that keeps air flowing.

### Why it suits a kasbah

The thick kasbah walls already provide thermal mass that keeps interiors cool; cooling tubes enhance that effect with continuous fresh airflow, and solar chimneys above the roofs make it more effective still as hot air naturally rises and escapes.

In practice, intake vents are subtly integrated at the base of the kasbah walls, camouflaged within garden elements. The underground tubes run beneath courtyards or gardens, using the earth's stable temperature to condition the air. Distribution vents deliver fresh, cooled air into residential rooms, while roof openings, windows or towers allow hot air to escape so the circulation stays continuous.

The system is essentially energy-free, needing no electricity apart from perhaps small fans to boost airflow. Beyond the energy saving, continuous natural airflow reduces pollutants, moisture buildup and the feeling of stale air. It also avoids the thermal shock common with air conditioning: instead of sudden blasts of cold air, spaces cool gradually, creating a smoother, healthier adaptation to temperature change.
